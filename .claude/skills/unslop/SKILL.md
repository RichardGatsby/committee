# Unslop

Everything this project emits gets read by people who did not ask for a robot's
opinion: committee members reading a verdict, whoever picks up the repo next.
Prose that smells generated gets skimmed, and a skimmed verdict is a wasted one.

Two passes. **Diagnose, then rewrite.** Do not edit while reading; you will fix
three tells and leave the structure intact, which is the part that actually
reads as machine-written.

## Pass 1: diagnose

Read the draft and list the tells. Do not change anything yet.

### Phrases to cut outright

Openers that say nothing: *In today's fast-paced world*, *It's worth noting
that*, *It's important to understand*, *Let's dive in*, *At its core*, *When it
comes to*, *In order to* (say "to").

Throat-clearing before the point: *Essentially*, *Basically*, *Fundamentally*,
*Ultimately*, *Notably*, *Importantly*, *Interestingly*, *Simply put*.

Hedges stacked on hedges: *may potentially*, *can often sometimes*, *it seems
likely that perhaps*. One hedge is honest. Two is cowardice.

Closers that add nothing: *In conclusion*, *Overall*, *All in all*, *I hope this
helps*, *Let me know if you'd like me to*.

### Structures that give it away

- **The "not X, but Y" seesaw.** *It's not just a script, it's a framework.*
  *This isn't about winning — it's about consistency.* One per document is a
  rhetorical choice. Three is a tic.
- **Rule of three everywhere.** *Fast, simple, and reliable.* Real writing has
  lists of one, two, four and seven in it.
- **Every paragraph the same length.** Three sentences, three sentences, three
  sentences. Vary it. A one-sentence paragraph lands.
- **Every sentence the same length.** Read it aloud. If it has a metronome in
  it, break one sentence in half and run two others together.
- **Bulleted lists where a sentence would do.** A three-item list of short
  phrases is a sentence wearing a costume.
- **Bold on the first phrase of every bullet**, mechanically, whether or not
  that phrase is the point.
- **Headings for two sentences of content.**
- **Restating the question before answering it.**
- **A summary of what you just said**, immediately after saying it.

### Word-level tells

- Adverbs propping up weak verbs: *significantly improved*, *dramatically
  reduced*, *carefully considered*. Cut the adverb or change the verb.
- Inanimate things acting: *the data suggests*, *this approach ensures*, *the
  script empowers you to*. Data does not suggest; you concluded.
- Passive voice hiding who did it: *a bug was introduced*. By whom? Say so.
- Corporate verbs: *leverage*, *utilise*, *facilitate*, *streamline*,
  *empower*, *unlock*, *robust*, *seamless*, *comprehensive*, *delve*,
  *landscape*, *realm*, *tapestry*, *testament to*, *game-changer*,
  *best-in-class*.
- Vague quantities where a number exists: *significantly more accurate* when you
  know it is 63.9% against 63.0%.
- Emoji as section decoration.
- Curly quotes and ellipses in code, error strings or commit messages. Keep
  those ASCII: a `—` in an exception message is a machine fingerprint, and smart
  quotes break shell snippets people copy.

  The report output is the exception. `—` and `→` in the verdict line and the
  `MOVE DOWN: A → B` heading are deliberate, approved, and carry meaning the
  committee reads at a glance. Do not "fix" them.

## Pass 2: rewrite

Rebuild the passage rather than patching it.

**Lead with the finding.** The most useful sentence goes first, not after the
setup. *h2o wins 25 more games per 100 than his tier predicts* beats *Let's
take a look at what the data tells us about h2o.*

**Name the actor.** *I changed the threshold* or *the resolver rejects it*, not
*it was determined that* or *changes were made*.

**Use the number you have.** Vague comparatives are almost always a number you
did not bother to fetch. If you genuinely do not have it, say you do not have
it.

**Say the uncomfortable half.** If a verdict rests on 14 games, that belongs in
the same sentence as the verdict, not in a caveat three paragraphs down. Most
slop is generated by the urge to sound confident.

**Cut every sentence that survives its own deletion.** Delete it, read the
paragraph again. If nothing was lost, it stays deleted.

**Write the way you would say it to one person who knows the domain.** The
committee knows what a gather is. Do not explain it to them.

## Self-check

Score the passage out of 10 on each, and rewrite anything under 35 total:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Directness** | Does the first sentence carry the point? |
| **Rhythm** | Do sentence and paragraph lengths vary? |
| **Trust** | Does it explain things the reader already knows? |
| **Specificity** | Are the claims attached to numbers and names? |
| **Density** | What fraction of words could be deleted without loss? |

## Worked example

Before:

> It's worth noting that this tool leverages a comprehensive statistical
> approach to provide robust insights into player performance. By carefully
> analysing match data, it empowers the committee to make data-driven decisions.
> Ultimately, this isn't just about numbers — it's about fairness.

After:

> For each player the tool works out what their team's tier composition said
> should happen in every recent match, compares that to what actually happened,
> and states whether the tier they hold should change.

Four sentences of positioning became one sentence of content, and the reader now
knows what the thing does.
